\begin{abstract}
Vision-language models (VLMs) increasingly read text directly from images, including code-switched text that mixes two languages and two scripts. Whether a VLM processes a language with the same internal components when it arrives as text tokens or as a rendered image remains unknown. Recent mechanistic interpretability studies show that a small subset of attention heads has a disproportionately large effect on a multilingual language model's performance in a given language, but these studies consider text input only. We investigate whether such language-important heads are modality-invariant in four open VLMs with at most 3B parameters. For English, Spanish, and Hindi, we score every decoder head with a gradient-based head-importance method adapted from LAHIS when the same sentence is given as text, as a rendered image with a query, and as a rendered image alone. Head sharing depends on the model. Qwen2-VL-2B and SmolVLM recruit largely separate heads for text and image input, InternVL3-2B recruits nearly the same heads for Hindi only, and the measure is inconclusive for PaliGemma-3B. Linear probes show that all four vision encoders encode script identity from their first layer. Ablating the top heads while the models read Hindi-English code-switched images destroys generation in every model, while ablating random heads does not, indicating that these heads are necessary for coherent output. Heads of either language cause the same collapse, so the ablation does not isolate a role in language routing, and a script-change metric misreads the collapse as a change of language. Finally, head positions shared across models are largely explained by where each model places its top heads.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Vision-language models (VLMs) increasingly read text directly from pixels. Models now process rendered text as images \citep{rust2023pixel, tschannen2023clippo}, parse documents and screenshots without a separate OCR step \citep{kim2022donut, lee2023pix2struct}, and use dedicated OCR heads to read text embedded in images \citep{baek2025ocr}. This text is not always in a single language. Screenshots of social media posts and chat messages can contain code-switched text, which alternates between two languages within one sentence. A VLM must then identify both languages from pixels, without the token boundaries that a text-only model receives.

How VLMs internally identify and process languages that arrive as pixels is therefore an open question for interpretability. Recent mechanistic interpretability studies have begun identifying the components responsible for language processing in large language models (LLMs). In particular, \citet{liu2026lahis} show that a small subset of attention heads has a disproportionately large effect on a multilingual LLM's performance in a given language, and \citet{tang2024neurons} find that a model's proficiency in a language depends mainly on a small subset of neurons. While these findings provide evidence that language processing is localized, they are derived almost exclusively from text input. Consequently, it remains unclear whether the same heads process a language when it reaches the decoder as image tokens rather than text tokens.

This question matters for how far text-only interpretability findings transfer to VLMs. A VLM passes an image through a vision encoder and sends the resulting tokens to a language decoder, which also reads text tokens directly. If both input paths rely on the same heads, findings from text-only models apply to visual input. If they rely on different heads, the visual path requires its own analysis. Prior work on text in images does not answer this question. OCR heads have been studied on text-retrieval tasks rendered as images, not on code-switched text \citep{baek2025ocr}, and cross-lingual attention in VLMs has been studied without separating the text path from the image path \citep{ye2025claim}.

Motivated by this gap, we investigate the following research questions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1: Are language-important heads modality-invariant?} Does the same sentence recruit the same decoder attention heads when a VLM receives it as text tokens and as a rendered image?
    \item \textbf{RQ2: Are these heads causally involved in language routing?} When a VLM reads code-switched text from an image, does ablating the top heads change the language of its output beyond what ablating random heads does?
\end{itemize}

To answer these questions, we present each sentence in three input conditions (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}) and score every decoder head with a variant of LAHIS \citep{liu2026lahis} in four open VLMs from three decoder families. We further probe the vision encoders for script identity and ablate the top heads while the models read Hindi-English code-switched images. Code and data for reproducing our experiments will be released with the paper.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Mechanistic Interpretability of Language Processing}

Mechanistic interpretability has located several components that language models use to process multiple languages. \citet{liu2026lahis} introduce Language Attention Head Importance Scores (LAHIS), which rank attention heads with one forward and one backward pass through a soft attention head mask. They find that most heads contribute little to performance in a target language while a small subset has a disproportionately large effect, and they separate language-specific from language-general heads. \citet{tang2024neurons} identify language-specific neurons with language activation probability entropy. They find that a small subset of neurons, mostly in the top and bottom layers, accounts for a model's proficiency in a language, and that activating or deactivating these neurons steers the output language. \citet{wendler2024llamas} find that the middle layers of Llama-2 already decode the correct next token but favor its English version, which suggests an intermediate concept space closer to English. \citet{nie2025confusion} trace language-confusion errors in English-centric LLMs to specific token positions and late-layer transitions. All of these studies consider text input only, and none studies code-switching directly.

\subsection{Mechanistic Interpretability of Vision-Language Models}

Recent work has begun to trace how image information moves through the language model of a VLM. \citet{palit2023towards} adapt causal tracing to BLIP, and \citet{basu2024understanding} extend causal information tracing to multimodal LLMs to locate where visual information is stored and transferred. \citet{neo2025towards} track how visual token representations change across the layers of LLaVA's language model. Closer to our setting, \citet{ye2025claim} identify cross-lingual attention misalignment in VLMs and intervene on it to reduce multilingual object hallucination. They study monolingual queries in several languages and do not separate the text path from the image path. \citet{baek2025ocr} identify OCR heads that extract text from images in Qwen2-VL and InternVL2, using image versions of passkey and needle-in-a-haystack retrieval tasks. Their tasks do not involve code-switched or mixed-script text.

\subsection{Reading Text from Pixels}

Several models process rendered text as pixels \citep{rust2023pixel, tschannen2023clippo} or read documents and screenshots without an OCR step \citep{kim2022donut, lee2023pix2struct}. Within CLIP, the image encoder matches images of words with images of the scenes they describe, and its spelling ability can be isolated in a separate subspace \citep{materzynska2022disentangling}. Many attention heads in CLIP's image encoder also have property-specific roles, such as location or shape \citep{gandelsman2024interpreting}.

Building upon this line of work, we investigate whether the attention heads that matter for a language in a VLM's decoder are the same when the language arrives as text and as a rendered image. Specifically, we score every decoder head in both input paths, probe the vision encoder for script identity, and test the top heads with ablation on code-switched images.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Attention Heads in the Decoder}

Each decoder layer $\ell$ contains a multi-head self-attention module with $H$ heads. Head $h$ produces an output $\mathbf{z}_{\ell,h} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h}$ at every token position. The module concatenates the head outputs and applies an output projection $\mathbf{W}^{O}_{\ell}$,
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{MHA}_{\ell}(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{W}^{O}_{\ell} \left[ \mathbf{z}_{\ell,1}; \dots; \mathbf{z}_{\ell,H} \right].
\end{equation}
Because the head outputs are still separate at the input of $\mathbf{W}^{O}_{\ell}$, each head's contribution can be scaled or removed exactly at this point. In a VLM, the decoder applies the same heads to text tokens and to the image tokens produced by the vision encoder and its connector.

\subsection{Head Importance}

LAHIS \citep{liu2026lahis} attaches a soft mask value $m_{\ell,h}$ to each head and scales its output,
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{MHA}_{\ell}(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{m}) = \mathbf{W}^{O}_{\ell} \left[ m_{\ell,1}\,\mathbf{z}_{\ell,1}; \dots; m_{\ell,H}\,\mathbf{z}_{\ell,H} \right].
\end{equation}
LAHIS scores a head by the expected value of $\left| m_{\ell,h}\, \partial \mathcal{L} / \partial m_{\ell,h} \right|$ over a corpus in one language, where $\mathcal{L}$ is the language-modeling loss, and counts only negative gradients. We use a simplified variant. With all mask values set to one, the importance of head $h$ in layer $\ell$ is the gradient magnitude
\begin{equation}
    I_{\ell,h} = \left| \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial m_{\ell,h}} \right|_{\mathbf{m}=\mathbf{1}},
\end{equation}
averaged over a set of sentences in one language, without the restriction to negative gradients. The loss is the standard next-token loss and does not target language identity. A head therefore scores high if masking it changes the loss on text in that language, which includes heads with language-general functions. \citet{liu2026lahis} also find language-general heads with their method.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

We conduct all experiments on four open VLMs with at most 3B parameters, chosen to differ in vision encoder, decoder family, and image-token compression. The model size keeps gradient attribution over every head within our compute budget.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Qwen2-VL-2B-Instruct} \citep{wang2024qwen2vl} pairs a custom 2D-RoPE vision transformer (0.67B) and a $2{\times}2$ patch merger with a Qwen2-1.5B decoder (28 layers, 12 heads per layer).
    \item \textbf{InternVL3-2B} \citep{zhu2025internvl3} pairs InternViT-300M-448px-V2.5 and pixel-shuffle compression with a Qwen2.5-1.5B decoder (28 layers, 12 heads per layer).
    \item \textbf{SmolVLM-Instruct} \citep{marafioti2025smolvlm} pairs a SigLIP-SO400M encoder \citep{zhai2023sigmoid} and a pixel-shuffle connector with a SmolLM2-1.7B decoder (24 layers, 32 heads per layer).
    \item \textbf{PaliGemma-3B (mix-448)} \citep{beyer2024paligemma} pairs a SigLIP-SO400M encoder at 448 pixels and a linear projector that keeps all 1,024 image tokens with a Gemma-2B decoder (18 layers, 8 heads per layer, multi-query attention). PaliGemma-3B is a prefix language model, so we score its decoder on the text after the prefix \texttt{[BOS]\,\textbackslash n}.
\end{itemize}

Qwen2-VL-2B and InternVL3-2B use decoders from the same family. Qwen2.5 was pretrained on 18 trillion tokens, against 7 trillion for Qwen2 \citep{qwen2024qwen25}. The Qwen2.5 report does not state whether Qwen2.5 was initialized from Qwen2 weights. SmolVLM uses a Llama-architecture SmolLM2 decoder, and PaliGemma-3B uses a Gemma decoder, each from a separate pretraining lineage.

\paragraph{Input Conditions.}
Following Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}, we present each sentence in three conditions. In condition A (text), the decoder reads the raw sentence and the vision encoder does not run. In condition B (image+query), the full VLM reads the rendered sentence with the fixed query ``Read this:'' and the loss is computed on the sentence as the target. Condition C (image-only) is identical to B except that the query is empty. Comparing B with C isolates the effect of the query, and comparing A with C isolates the effect of modality. Condition A uses the decoder taken from inside the VLM after vision-language training, not the original pretrained checkpoint. This keeps the comparison between A and C free of differences caused by vision-language training.

\paragraph{Dataset Construction.}
All code-switched text is real and human-produced, with gold token-level language labels. Hindi-English sentences come from the Devanagari-script Twitter portion of the GLUECoS part-of-speech corpus \citep{khanuja2020gluecos}. Spanish-English sentences come from the code-switched portion of the Bangor Miami corpus of transcribed speech \citep{deuchar2014building}. Monolingual English, Spanish, and Hindi sentences come from FLORES-200 \citep{nllb2022}. We apply Penn Treebank detokenization, filters for script consistency and noise, and a two-stage fluency filter that first applies heuristic checks and then removes the quartile with the highest Qwen2.5-0.5B perplexity in each language pair. A content filter removes profanity and political material, judged by Claude against a fixed rubric. The final corpus contains 806 Hindi-English sentences from the GLUECoS training and development splits, 127 Spanish-English sentences, 750 monolingual sentences (250 per language), and 100 transliteration controls, which are Hindi sentences written in Latin script.

Every sentence is kept as raw text and rendered as black text on a white image, with each token drawn in a font for its script (Noto Sans for Latin and Noto Sans Devanagari for Devanagari). We record a bounding box for every token, which gives each image patch a script label. Figure~\ref{fig:stimuli} shows examples.

\subsection{Head Overlap}
\label{sec:overlap_method}

For each model, language, and condition, we take the 20 highest-scoring heads and compute the Jaccard overlap between conditions. The chance level is the 95th percentile of the overlap between random 20-head sets, over 10{,}000 draws from each model's grid. We repeat the analysis for $K \in \{5, 10, 20, 40, 80\}$ heads. To compare models, we count the (layer, head) positions that two models' text-condition sets share.

\subsection{Script Probes}
\label{sec:probe_method}

To test whether script identity is available before the decoder, we train a linear probe at every vision-encoder layer to predict whether an image patch shows Latin or Devanagari script. Patch labels come from the token bounding boxes, mapped onto the grid of image tokens that the decoder receives after any patch merger or pixel shuffle. We exclude background patches, so chance accuracy is 0.5. We sample 100 patches per image and 4,000 text patches per layer. Each probe is a class-balanced logistic regression, evaluated by balanced accuracy with stratified 5-fold cross-validation over patches.

\subsection{Head Ablation}
\label{sec:ablation_method}

Gradient importance does not establish that a head affects generation. To test this, we set the output of a head set to zero and generate from 100 Hindi-English code-switched images, with greedy decoding and a limit of 64 tokens. The ablated sets are the 20 highest-scoring text-condition heads for English and for Hindi, which we call English heads and Hindi heads. These sets are not contrasted between languages. We compare against two controls. The random-head control ablates 20 heads drawn from outside both top-20 sets. Mean ablation replaces each head's output with its mean over token positions in the same image's un-ablated forward pass. We run the ablation once and then three more times with new seeds, each drawing its own 100 sentences and its own random heads.

We measure two outcomes. The first is the script label of each output (Latin, Devanagari, other, or none for output without letters), where a script change is any change of label from the un-ablated output. The second is fluency. An ablated output counts as fluent if it keeps at least half of the words of the un-ablated output for the same image, and we read this coarse proxy against the random-head control. For Spanish-English, which uses one script, we run a language identifier (\texttt{langid}) on the output instead.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We examine whether language-important heads are modality-invariant from four complementary perspectives. We first test whether the vision encoders make script identity available to the decoder. We then compare the top heads across input conditions (RQ1). Next, we ablate the top heads while the models read code-switched images (RQ2). Finally, we compare head positions across models.

\subsection{Availability: Script identity is decodable from the first vision-encoder layer}
\label{sec:probes}

We first ask whether the image path carries script identity at all. If it did not, a difference between text and image head sets would be expected. Instead, all four vision encoders separate Latin from Devanagari patches above chance from layer 0 (Figure~\ref{fig:probes}, Table~\ref{tab:probes}). Accuracy varies widely across encoders. InternVL3-2B and PaliGemma-3B reach near-perfect accuracy, Qwen2-VL-2B plateaus near 0.81, and SmolVLM stays near 0.63 at every depth.

The same encoder family can yield very different results. PaliGemma-3B and SmolVLM both use SigLIP-SO400M but sit at opposite ends. PaliGemma-3B runs the encoder at 448 pixels and keeps all 1,024 patch tokens, while SmolVLM passes it lower-resolution, pooled tokens. Because all models receive the same images and fonts, this deployment difference is a likely cause. The probes also track script rather than language. Applied to the transliteration controls, each model's best-layer probe labels most of them Latin, at rates at or above its accuracy on code-switched images. These results indicate that script identity is available to every decoder, so differences between text and image head sets do not come from a missing script signal.

\subsection{Modality: Head sharing between text and image input depends on the model}
\label{sec:overlap}

We next ask whether the same sentence recruits the same heads as text and as an image. Figure~\ref{fig:overlap} compares, for each model and language, the overlap between the text and image-only head sets ($\AcapC$) with the overlap between the two image conditions ($\BcapC$). If the decoder processed a language with the same heads regardless of modality, text-image overlap would approach image-image overlap. Instead, the four models show three distinct patterns (full values in Appendix~\ref{app:overlap}).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/overlap_dumbbell.pdf}
\caption{Jaccard overlap of the top 20 heads between input conditions. Circles show text versus image-only overlap ($\AcapC$), and squares show image+query versus image-only overlap ($\BcapC$). Shading marks each model's chance level. Text and image inputs use separate heads in Qwen2-VL-2B and SmolVLM, nearly the same heads in InternVL3-2B for Hindi, and an unresolved pattern in PaliGemma-3B.}
\label{fig:overlap}
\end{figure}

In Qwen2-VL-2B and SmolVLM, text-image overlap stays low for every language, while the two image conditions overlap much more. Once an image is present, these models recruit a consistent head set with or without a query, and that set differs from the head set for the same sentence as text. InternVL3-2B shows the opposite pattern for Hindi only. Its Hindi text-image overlap reaches 0.667, well above its English and Spanish overlaps. Hindi competence does not explain this result, because Qwen2-VL-2B models Hindi at least as well (Table~\ref{tab:competence}) and keeps its heads separate. For PaliGemma-3B, all overlaps lie between 0.21 and 0.33, modestly above its chance level of 0.143. Its grid has only 144 heads, so a top-20 set covers a large fraction of it and Jaccard overlap loses resolution. These patterns hold for every $K$ from 5 to 80 (Appendix~\ref{app:ksweep}).

The layer placement of the top heads mirrors these patterns (Figure~\ref{fig:layers}). Qwen2-VL-2B places almost all of its text-condition heads in layers 0--1 but few of its image-condition heads there. InternVL3-2B also places its text-condition heads early, and for Hindi its image-condition heads follow them into the same layers, matching its shared Hindi heads. SmolVLM places its text-condition heads partly early and its image-condition heads mostly later, while PaliGemma-3B places few heads in layers 0--1 in any condition.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/layer_placement.pdf}
\caption{Number of each top-20 head set that lies in decoder layers 0--1, by model, language, and condition. Text-condition heads concentrate in the first two layers in both Qwen-family models, while image-condition heads mostly lie later. InternVL3-2B's Hindi image-condition heads are the exception. The dashed line shows the count expected if the 20 heads were spread evenly over the grid.}
\label{fig:layers}
\end{figure}

Together, these results indicate that language-important heads are not modality-invariant in general. Whether text and image inputs share heads depends on the model and, for InternVL3-2B, on the language. A strong script signal in the encoder does not by itself lead to shared heads. InternVL3-2B and PaliGemma-3B have the strongest script signals, but only InternVL3-2B shares heads, although PaliGemma-3B's inconclusive overlap makes this comparison weak.

\subsection{Necessity: The top heads are required for coherent generation}
\label{sec:ablation}

Head overlap alone does not establish that the identified heads affect generation. To evaluate their causal role, we ablate each model's top 20 heads while it reads Hindi-English code-switched images. Zero-ablating the top heads for either language destroys generation in every model (Figure~\ref{fig:fluency}). At most 1\% of outputs stay fluent, against 71--76\% for the random-head control. The collapse takes a different form in each model. PaliGemma-3B mostly returns empty strings, Qwen2-VL-2B returns output without letters or with fragments of Chinese, Japanese, or Korean (CJK) characters, InternVL3-2B's Hindi-head ablations contain CJK characters in every output, and SmolVLM repeats a special token. No un-ablated output contains CJK characters. Mean ablation also destroys generation, except for SmolVLM's Hindi heads (Appendix~\ref{app:ablation}).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fluency_bars.pdf}
\caption{Fluency under head ablation on Hindi-English code-switched images. Bars show the percentage of 400 generations that keep at least half of the un-ablated output's words. Zero-ablating the top 20 heads for either language destroys generation in every model, while ablating 20 random heads leaves most outputs fluent.}
\label{fig:fluency}
\end{figure}

These results show that the top heads are necessary for coherent output, and that a random set of the same size is not. They do not show that these heads route language. English heads and Hindi heads cause the same collapse, and the two sets largely coincide (Section~\ref{sec:lineage}). In Qwen2-VL-2B they include 19 or 20 of the 24 heads in layers 0--1, so the collapse may reflect general damage to the first layers. Testing language routing requires a random control drawn from the same layers and head sets contrasted between languages. We therefore answer RQ2 in the negative for language routing and in the positive for necessity.

The ablation also exposes a failure of the script-change metric. Output without letters changes the script label and therefore counts as a change of script. In the single run, all 74 script changes after ablating Qwen2-VL-2B's Hindi heads were outputs that lost all letters, and so were all 97 for PaliGemma-3B. InternVL3-2B shows a low script-change rate because its collapsed outputs keep enough Latin letters to retain a Latin label. Differences in script-change rate between models therefore reflect how each model fails rather than how it routes language (Appendix~\ref{app:ablation}). Without ablation, SmolVLM produces Devanagari output for 37 of 100 images, against 5 to 15 for the other models. Competence does not explain this difference, because SmolVLM has the highest Hindi perplexity (Table~\ref{tab:competence}).

\subsection{Cross-model consistency: Shared head positions follow layer placement}
\label{sec:lineage}

Finally, we ask whether the top heads occupy the same positions across models. Within each model, most top text-condition heads are shared across languages. The number of heads in the top 20 for all three languages is 18 for Qwen2-VL-2B, 14 for InternVL3-2B, 14 for SmolVLM, and 15 for PaliGemma-3B. Our scores therefore mostly rank language-general heads, which is also why the English and Hindi sets in Section~\ref{sec:ablation} largely coincide.

Across models, the two Qwen-family models share the most head positions (Table~\ref{tab:crossmodel}). The other pairs share far fewer, and SmolVLM and PaliGemma-3B share none. Layer placement predicts the Qwen-pair overlap. Both models place most of their top text-condition heads in layers 0--1, which hold 24 heads in each model. If each model placed those heads at random within the two layers, the sets would share about 11, 15, and 15 positions for English, Spanish, and Hindi, close to the observed 13, 16, and 15.

A shared head position carries no meaning beyond layer placement unless the two models share initialization, because heads within a layer are interchangeable across independently trained models. The Qwen2.5 report does not state whether Qwen2.5 was initialized from Qwen2 weights, and the other pairs come from separate lineages. These results indicate a layer-level pattern rather than head-level correspondence. With four models in which decoder family varies together with head count, grid shape, attention type, and image compression, our data cannot show whether this pattern follows decoder family.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We investigated whether the attention heads that matter for a language in a VLM's decoder are the same when the language arrives as text tokens and as a rendered image. Across four small VLMs, we found that the answer depends on the model. Qwen2-VL-2B and SmolVLM recruit largely separate heads for the two inputs, and Qwen2-VL-2B's text heads concentrate in the first two decoder layers. InternVL3-2B recruits nearly the same heads for Hindi only, and the measure is inconclusive for PaliGemma-3B. Script probes showed that every vision encoder encodes script identity from its first layer, so these differences do not come from a missing script signal. Ablation showed that the top heads are necessary for coherent generation on code-switched images, but it did not isolate a role in language routing, and a script-change metric misread the resulting collapse as a change of language. Together, these findings indicate that language-head findings from text-only models do not necessarily transfer to the visual input path of VLMs, and that head-ablation studies need a check on output quality. Future work may score heads directly on code-switched text, contrast heads between languages, and extend the analysis to larger VLMs and to language pairs that separate script from language family.

\section*{Limitations}

Our study covers four VLMs with at most 3B parameters and two language pairs. While this setting allows gradient attribution over every head, it remains unclear whether the observed patterns extend to larger VLMs. With only Hindi-English and Spanish-English, we also cannot tell whether InternVL3-2B's Hindi-specific overlap depends on script or on language family.

We identify heads on monolingual sentences, following \citet{liu2026lahis}. RQ1 therefore compares text and image processing of single-language content, and code-switched text enters only through the probes and the ablation. Scoring heads on code-switched sentences, with the loss restricted to one language's tokens, would link the head sets directly to code-switched input.

Our ablations use absolute top-20 head sets, a random control drawn from the whole network, and a coarse fluency proxy. These choices establish that the heads are necessary for coherent output, but they cannot establish what role the heads play in routing language. A layer-matched control and head sets contrasted between languages remain an important direction for future work.

Finally, the script-probe folds split patches rather than images, so patches from one image can appear in both training and test data, which likely inflates the reported accuracies. The transliteration check uses new images and is not affected. The head-overlap values come from a single run, and decoder family varies together with grid shape, head count, attention type, and image compression, so we cannot attribute a difference between models to any one of these factors.
